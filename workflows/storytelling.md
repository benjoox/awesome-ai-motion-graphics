# Storytelling for motion graphics

Use this guide before the [director's brief](../prompts/director-brief.md) when planning an explainer, product film, data story, or narrative animation. It connects what the viewer needs to understand with what each scene shows. Ambient loops and visual studies may need continuity and composition without a conflict or plot.

## Source and attribution

Emma Coats's storytelling advice is widely circulated as “Pixar's 22 rules.” [Aerogramme Writers' Studio's transcription](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/) credits her original tweets as a Pixar story artist. That attribution does not establish an official studio policy or a guarantee of audience response.

The checklist and example below are original applications to motion graphics. Read the linked list for Coats's wording; this guide does not reproduce the poster or the full list.

## Eight reminders

1. **Define the takeaway.** Write one sentence describing what this audience should understand or feel afterward. Use that sentence to assess visual choices.
2. **Give viewers a reason to care.** Establish a relevant question, obstacle, or consequence. Use supplied evidence; never invent urgency, customer pain, or product claims.
3. **Plan the payoff.** Sketch the final state or answer before building the middle. For a teaching film, decide what the viewer should now be able to explain.
4. **Connect the beats.** Make each scene develop, demonstrate, or resolve the previous idea. A transition can connect pictures without explaining their relationship.
5. **Reduce competing ideas.** Remove scenes, copy, and effects that distract from the takeaway. Keep labels, context, and reading time needed for understanding.
6. **Explore alternatives.** Sketch several ways to show the mechanism or change. Compare a diagram, a concrete example, or a visual metaphor before choosing; keep metaphors consistent with the facts.
7. **Test a rough version.** Render a timed animatic with planned text and any narration. Check whether the sequence makes sense before polishing motion and sound.
8. **Revise toward the message.** After reviewing the whole sequence, align the opening and payoff with what it actually communicates. Fix consequential problems, then finish against the brief's delivery criteria.

These are planning choices, not a requirement for fast cuts, a character in every scene, or an action on every musical beat.

## Worked example: from a feature list to an explanation

This hypothetical 30-second film teaches how grouping tasks by status works. Assume the supplied specification supports only creating a task, assigning one of three statuses, and grouping tasks by that status. Do not infer automation, integrations, or time savings.

**Audience:** someone unfamiliar with the board. **Takeaway:** a task's status determines its group. **Payoff:** the viewer can predict where a task will appear after its status changes.

A feature list could show three disconnected title cards: “Create tasks,” “Assign status,” and “Group tasks.” Instead, follow one labeled task through the mechanism:

| Time | Visual beat | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 s | Show three task cards in a list; ask where a task marked “In progress” belongs. | Establish the question using a concrete example. |
| 5–11 s | Focus on one card and its status field; set it to “In progress.” | Show the input that controls placement. |
| 11–19 s | Group the list into “To do,” “In progress,” and “Done”; keep the selected card identifiable as it appears in its group. | Explain the connection between status and position. |
| 19–25 s | Change that card's status to “Done” and show its new group. | Demonstrate the same relationship with a changed input. |
| 25–30 s | Hold the resulting board with the recap “Status determines the group.” | Resolve the opening question and leave time to read. |

Timing is a starting point for an animatic, not a measured reading-speed claim. Use an original schematic unless supplied product assets establish the actual interface and behavior. Adjust the holds after playback; extend the duration or reduce copy if comprehension needs more time.

## Apply and review

Bring source facts, exact approved copy, audience, duration, format, assets and rights, and any narration or music into the [director's brief](../prompts/director-brief.md). Record missing inputs instead of filling gaps with invented claims.

For each timed beat, specify its purpose, visible state, action, text, sound, and connection to the next beat. Render an animatic, then use [render review](../prompts/render-review.md) to inspect playback, transitions, readability, and audio together. A contact sheet alone cannot establish pacing or comprehension.

Where possible, ask a viewer unfamiliar with the subject to describe the takeaway and answer the opening question. Record misunderstandings and timestamped changes. Self-review can identify defects but does not establish audience understanding.

Deliver the takeaway sentence, timed beat sheet, source and asset references, animatic, review notes, and final video with source and render instructions. Describe unresolved limitations alongside the result.
